# Narrative choices that change what a player can do

Five games offer five different places to look for a consequence. A reply can become available mid-conversation, a missing companion can change a later scene, a campaign can carry preparation forward, and the surroundings can change the value of a power or the shape of a route. The design questions below are hypotheses to try in an original prototype. They do not report measured player reactions.

## Mass Effect (2007) — choosing a conversational intent

The manual for the first game’s PC release describes a consistent spatial vocabulary. Left-side responses generally explore a subject, while right-side responses tend to advance the exchange. Investing in Charm or Intimidate can reveal additional responses. Players can select a response before Shepard’s turn to speak. That last detail separates the moment of choice from the moment of performance. [EA, *Mass Effect* PC manual, “Conversation”](https://eaassets-a.akamaihd.net/eahelp/manuals/mass-effect-manuals_PC_en_US.pdf)

**Design inference.** A player who wants more context should be able to distinguish asking a question from making a commitment. A short label can preserve conversational pace, but its eventual performance can imply a stronger promise than the player intended.

**Original experiment.** At a municipal loading desk, offer a question about storage and a commitment to reserve a lift. Write your own labels, lines and layout. Before playing the response, ask an authorized tester which choice would close the negotiation. After the response, ask what they believe they promised.

## Mass Effect 2 (2010) — a later scene reads the crew’s history

Lead cinematic animator Parrish Ley describes how the team built the finale around different available squadmates, loyalty-mission histories and possible losses. Even assigning a line to a speaker depended on which characters could actually be there. BioWare’s account describes the production problem and its repeated tests. [BioWare, “The Effect on Us”](https://blog.bioware.com/2013/11/07/the-effect-on-us/)

**Design inference.** A relationship can matter when someone takes responsibility at a critical moment. A generic thank-you line gives the player less to connect to their earlier investment than a visible change in who can help. An absent character also needs a coherent version of the scene.

**Original experiment.** A festival has one overnight rehearsal slot. Give it to either the interpreters or the riggers. Write the next morning’s opening with a different person doing a different job in each case. Record who is present, who can perform each task, and how the scene proceeds if neither crew rehearsed.

## Mass Effect 3 (2012) — bringing preparation into a shared outcome

For the original release, producer Jesse Houston explained that earlier campaign decisions fed into Galaxy at War. He distinguished those decisions from the Galactic Readiness level affected by co-op play. For *Legendary Edition*, BioWare removed the multiplayer and companion-app influences and rebalanced preparation across the trilogy’s included content. A formula or threshold from one edition therefore needs its edition label. [BioWare, interview with Jesse Houston](https://blog.bioware.com/2011/11/04/my-interview-with-mass-effect-3-producer-jesse-houston/) · [EA and BioWare, “Gameplay Calibrations”](https://www.ea.com/ea-play/news/gameplay-calibrations)

**Design inference.** A common finale can acknowledge many earlier decisions without giving each decision its own ending. If contributions disappear into a total, players may struggle to identify which earlier act caused a particular benefit.

**Original experiment.** Give a fictional district two ways to prepare for a cold night. A repaired greenhouse provides shelter and a kept promise brings a delivery cart. Show the contributing facts at the final depot. Test a fresh campaign and one with inherited commitments. Ask the player which actions made each service possible.

## The Darkness (2007) — reading an ability in its surroundings

In Starbreeze’s original game, the manual links Jackie’s power to the lighting around him. Shadows provide energy and light drains it. Markings on the manifested heads indicate the selected power and remaining energy through their color and length. The player has both an environmental cue and visible information about the current resource. [Starbreeze, original game credits](https://www.starbreeze.com/our-work/the-darkness/) · [2K, original Xbox 360 manual, “Playing the Game” and “The Darkness”, pp. 8–11, scan hosted by Manuall](https://manuall.co.uk/microsoft-xbox-360-the-darkness/)

**Design inference.** A place can alter a player’s options through the normal ability rules. If the surroundings are difficult to read, a drained resource and an unavailable activation point can feel like the same unexplained refusal.

**Original experiment.** Give a pneumatic freight yard a limited pressure supply. A valve pedestal shows where a layout change is possible. A needle and a numeric readout show whether the change is affordable. Try the control once away from a pedestal and once at an empty tank. Check whether the two refusals communicate different next actions without relying on color alone.

## Legacy of Kain: Soul Reaver (1999) — recognizing a changed place

Director and writer Amy Hennig describes how the original team stored alternate vertex positions and lighting values for the material and spectral realms. Changing realm could alter the geometry a player used to negotiate an obstacle or solve a puzzle. This account concerns the 1999 game. [PlayStation Blog, interview with Amy Hennig](https://blog.playstation.com/2012/10/12/behind-the-classics-amy-hennig-talks-soul-reaver-secrets/)

**Design inference.** Reusing an identifiable landmark gives players a point of comparison. A route change becomes intelligible when they can see what used to connect, what moved and what now lines up.

**Original experiment.** Build a scale model of a canal museum where a counterweight raises two exhibits at once. A numbered clock stays in place in both layouts. Draw the walkable connections before and after the shift, test them in both directions, and ask a player to predict the next accessible door before they move.

## Studio practice

The **reference-narrative** Lab combines three of these techniques in an original freight-dispatch exercise. An earlier promise selects a recipient, a pressure valve changes the yard’s layout, and a legal handoff depends on both. Predict every command, including a refused one. The evaluator also explores alternative legal routes to find shortcuts that would erase the promise. Inspect the playable scene separately for believable character motivation, readable controls and a visible payoff.
